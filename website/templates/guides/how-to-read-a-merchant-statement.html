The short version: ignore the rates printed on your statement for a moment. Find two numbers: total fees and total card takings. Divide the first by the second. That's your effective rate, what you really pay per pound taken. Everything else on the statement explains where it came from.

What a merchant statement is

Your merchant statement is the monthly bill from the company that processes your card payments. It lists what you took on card and every fee charged against it. It usually comes as a PDF by email or in an online portal, and it's often several pages long. That's not an accident: the more pages, the less likely you are to read it.

The five sections on most UK statements

  1. Header. Your business name, the statement period and your merchant ID (MID), the number that identifies your account.
  2. Settlements or deposits. The money paid into your bank, usually day by day. Sometimes fees are taken from each deposit; sometimes they're collected once a month by direct debit.
  3. Card summary. Your takings split by card type: consumer debit, consumer credit, business cards, and sometimes non-UK cards.
  4. Transaction charges. The percentage charged on each card type, and any per-transaction fee.
  5. Other or fixed charges. Rental, PCI fees, minimum monthly charges, and VAT on the parts that carry it.

What to notice:

  • The debit rate looks low, at 1.29%. But fixed fees and VAT add another £62.16 a month.
  • The business card line is small but charged at double the debit rate. Business cards have no fee cap. Our interchange guide explains why.
  • VAT is applied only to the fixed fees. The percentage charges are VAT-exempt.

Work out your effective rate in two minutes

  1. Find total charges for the month, including VAT. Here: £234.32.
  2. Find total card takings for the same month. Here: £12,400.
  3. Divide, then multiply by 100: £234.32 ÷ £12,400 × 100 = 1.89%.

So this café pays 1.89% overall, well above its "1.29%" headline rate. That's £2,811.84 a year. If fees come off each deposit instead of in one bill, add up the fees column for the month instead.

Red flags

  • "Non-compliance" fees. You're being charged because a security questionnaire wasn't done. This is usually fixable in under an hour. See our PCI guide.
  • A minimum monthly service charge. In quieter months, you pay for fees you didn't run up.
  • Rates that have crept up. Compare this statement with one from a year ago. Some contracts allow rises with notice.
  • Separate authorisation fees on top of a "low" percentage, especially if your sales are small.
  • High business or non-UK card rates. Fine if those cards are rare. Expensive if they're a big share of your takings.
  • Charges you don't recognise. "Service", "account" or "statement" fees. Ask what each one is for.

What's a good effective rate?

It depends on your size, your average sale and your card mix, so there's no single right number. As a rough guide for a small UK business taking cards in person:

  • Above 1.75% is close to what a pay-as-you-go reader costs, so a contract at this level is usually worth challenging.
  • Between 1% and 1.75% is common, and there's often still room to cut.
  • Below 1% is usually only available to businesses with good volumes and mostly debit cards.
